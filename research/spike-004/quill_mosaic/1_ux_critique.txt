# Mosaic response

**Task:** I have a settings page with 12 toggles. Users complain it is overwhelming. What should I do?


## ux_diagnosis_and_grouping *(voice: claude)*

## Why 12 toggles feels overwhelming

It's rarely the *number* — it's that every toggle is presented as equally important, equally consequential, and equally cryptic. Specifically:

1. **Flat hierarchy.** A 12-item list with no visual grouping forces the user to read all 12 before deciding what's relevant. Cognitive load is O(n) when it should be O(log n) via grouping. Miller's 7±2 is the usual cite, but the real issue is *scanning cost* — without sections, the user can't skip past things they don't care about.
2. **Unclear consequences.** "Enable telemetry" — does that mean crash reports? Usage analytics? Will my data leave the device? When users can't predict the outcome of a toggle, they freeze. They either flip everything off (and break the product) or leave everything alone (and miss features that would help them).
3. **No defaults signal.** If the ship-state is already correct for 80% of users, the UI is asking them to re-make 12 decisions someone has already made well. The toggles should *feel* like "things you can change if you want," not "things you must decide."
4. **No notion of which ones matter.** A toggle for "dark mode" sits next to a toggle for "experimental WebGL renderer" with equal visual weight. The frequent/safe knob and the rare/dangerous knob look identical.

## Information architecture proposal

**Group into 3 clusters** (2 is too coarse, 4+ starts to recreate the original problem):

- **Appearance & behavior** — the stuff users touch monthly (theme, density, language, notifications). Visible by default, near the top.
- **Privacy & data** — things with consequences users care about (telemetry, crash reports, sync). Visible by default, but with a one-line plain-language consequence under each label: *"Sends anonymized crash reports to help us fix bugs. No personal data."*
- **Advanced** — collapsed by default, behind a disclosure. Power-user stuff: experimental features, dev tools, low-level overrides. Probably 4–6 of your 12 toggles live here.

Add a **search box** at the top once you have grouping — users who know the toggle's name shouldn't have to hunt.

## Presets vs. toggles

Look for toggles that *co-vary*. If "reduce motion," "high contrast," and "larger text" are almost always flipped together, that's not three toggles — that's an **Accessibility preset** ("Standard" / "High contrast") with an "Customize" escape hatch underneath. Same for privacy: a "Minimal data sharing" preset can flip 3–4 related toggles atomically, and the individual toggles remain available for users who want fine-grained control. The rule of thumb: if you'd struggle to write distinct one-line consequences for two toggles, they probably belong in one preset.

## Communicating impact

Every visible toggle needs a **subtitle in plain language** describing what changes when it's on, in concrete user-observable terms — not implementation terms. "Enable hardware acceleration" → *"Uses your GPU for smoother animations. May cause issues on older graphics drivers."* Frame consequences in terms of *what the user will see or experience*, and call out risk/reversibility when relevant. For destructive or sticky toggles ("Delete data on sign-out"), use a confirm step rather than relying on the toggle alone.

**One follow-up worth raising to the developer:** do you have any telemetry on which toggles users actually flip? If yes, the "surface the top 2–3" decision becomes data-driven instead of guesswork — and you may find that 4 of the 12 toggles are flipped by <1% of users and could be moved to Advanced or removed entirely. That's the single highest-leverage piece of input for this redesign.

## settings_schema_and_defaults *(voice: codex)*

I didn’t find a concrete app repo in `/private/tmp`, so I’d frame this as a portable settings contract the doer can adapt. The key move is to stop treating the 12 toggles as 12 independent booleans. Model them as a registry with ownership, defaults, visibility, dependencies, and conflict rules, then let the UI render either a simplified preset selector or a custom detail view from that registry.

```ts
type SettingId =
  | "notifications.email"
  | "notifications.push"
  | "notifications.digest"
  | "privacy.profileVisible"
  | "privacy.activityVisible"
  | "security.twoFactor"
  | "security.loginAlerts"
  | "automation.autoArchive"
  | "automation.smartSuggestions"
  | "display.compactMode"
  | "display.animations"
  | "beta.experimentalFeatures";

type SettingGroup = "notifications" | "privacy" | "security" | "automation" | "display" | "labs";
type DepStatus = "active" | "deprecated" | "hidden" | "removed";

type SettingSchema = {
  id: SettingId;
  group: SettingGroup;
  default: boolean;
  advanced: boolean;
  status: DepStatus;
  dependsOn?: Array<{ id: SettingId; value: boolean; cascade?: "disable" | "forceOff" | "forceOn" }>;
  conflictsWith?: Array<{ id: SettingId; when: boolean; resolve: "preferUser" | "preferSafety" | "forceOffThis" | "forceOffOther" }>;
  deprecatedBy?: SettingId;
  migrationNote?: string;
};
```

Presets should be named overlays, not separate state. Persist `{ mode: "simple" | "custom", overrides: Partial<Record<SettingId, boolean>>, schemaVersion }`. A preset computes effective values by starting from schema defaults, applying the preset overlay, then applying user overrides only in `custom` mode. For example: `Simple` could enable only the safest, highest-value defaults; `Recommended` could use product defaults; `Custom` preserves all user-chosen non-defaults. Do not store “simple toggle states” separately, because that creates drift. Store the selected mode plus explicit overrides, and derive the 12 booleans every time.

Migration should preserve intent without dragging every legacy user into Custom unnecessarily. On first load after the redesign, compare each legacy value to the new schema default. If all values match a preset, assign that preset and no overrides. If values differ only on advanced settings, assign `Simple` or `Recommended` plus hidden overrides, and surface “customized advanced settings” only when they open advanced controls. If any visible setting differs from the preset, assign `Custom` and carry over only non-default values. Deprecated settings should be mapped through `deprecatedBy` when possible; if no replacement exists, keep the old value in a read-only migration record for audit/rollback but exclude it from effective state.

Dependency and conflict resolution should be deterministic and testable. Compute effective settings in phases: defaults, preset overlay, user overrides, deprecation mapping, dependency cascades, conflict resolver. Dependency examples: if `notifications.email=false`, then `notifications.digest` should be `forceOff`; if `security.twoFactor=false`, `security.loginAlerts` may remain enabled but should not depend on 2FA unless the product truly requires it. Conflict examples: if `privacy.activityVisible=false` conflicts with `automation.smartSuggestions=true`, prefer privacy/safety and force suggestions off. Return both `effectiveSettings` and `resolutionEvents`, so the UI can explain “Smart suggestions were turned off because activity visibility is disabled” instead of silently flipping toggles.

One implementation detail I’d push for: put the schema in one typed registry and make the settings page, API validation, migration job, and tests all consume that same registry. The complaints are about UX overload, but the lasting fix is a coherent model where “advanced,” “deprecated,” “depends on,” and “conflicts with” are data, not scattered conditionals.

## copy_and_empty_states *(voice: claude)*

Here's a copy pass. I don't know the product, so I picked plausible toggles (a notes/writing app) — the doer can swap nouns; the voice and structure are the point. Verbs-and-outcomes, sentence case, no "please," helper text only when the consequence isn't obvious from the label.

**Page title:** Settings
**Subtitle (default/empty state, before any overrides):** You're on the defaults. Change anything here and we'll remember it on this account.

**Section: Your account**
- *Stay signed in on this device* — Skip the sign-in screen next time you open the app.
- *Require a second step to sign in* — Use an authenticator app whenever you sign in from somewhere new.

**Section: Writing**
- *Save as you type* — Drafts are saved every few seconds. No save button needed.
- *Check spelling* — Underline misspelled words while you write.
- *Suggest clearer phrasing* — Quiet suggestions appear in the margin. You decide whether to take them.
- *Sync across your devices* — Changes show up on your phone, tablet, and other browsers within a few seconds.

**Section: Appearance**
- *Match my system theme* — Switch to dark when your OS does. Turn this off to pick a theme yourself.
- *Reduce motion* — Skip the slide and fade animations.

**Section: Notifications**
- *Email me product updates* — A short note when we ship something worth knowing about. Roughly monthly.
- *Email me tips and offers* — Occasional ideas, deals, and survey invites.

**Advanced disclosure (collapsed by default):**
Label: **Advanced** — `▸` chevron, with sublabel "Beta features, diagnostics, and developer tools." Expanded label: "Advanced — these are off by default. Turn them on if you know what you're doing."
- *Share anonymous usage data* — Helps us see which features people actually use. No document contents, ever.
- *Send crash reports automatically* — When the app crashes, we get a stack trace. Nothing from your documents.
- *Try beta features* — Get early access to things we're still polishing. Expect rough edges.
- *Show keyboard shortcut hints* — Tiny shortcut labels appear next to menu items.

**Presets (above the toggle list, as a row of three cards):**
- **Default** — Most people leave it here. Sync on, drafts saved, theme follows your system.
- **Focused** — Same as Default, minus the writing suggestions and the email notifications. Quieter screen, quieter inbox.
- **Privacy-first** — Turns off usage data, crash reports, and marketing email. Sync stays on so your work follows you.

Below the cards: *Pick one to start. You can change individual toggles afterward — presets aren't locked.*

**Reorg notice (one-time, dismissible banner at top, for users with prior config):**
> We reorganized this page. Your settings carried over — nothing was turned on or off — but a few options moved into **Advanced**. Take a look when you have a minute. **Got it** · **Show what moved**

The "Show what moved" link opens a small dialog: *Three things moved into Advanced: usage data, crash reports, and beta features. Your choices for each are unchanged.*

**Reset to defaults — button label:** Reset to defaults
**Confirmation dialog:**
> **Reset all settings?**
> This puts every toggle back to its default. Your account, documents, and sign-in stay exactly as they are — only the preferences on this page change.
> [**Reset settings**] [Cancel]

**After reset (toast):** Settings reset. Undo (8s)

**Empty state for a fresh account (if you show this page before they've touched anything):**
> Nothing to tweak yet — the defaults are a good starting point. Come back here whenever something bugs you.

A note for synthesis: the helper text only fires where the label alone would leave a user guessing about a *consequence* (sync scope, what data leaves the device, what "beta" implies). I deliberately didn't write helper text under obvious ones like *Check spelling* — adding it there is what makes settings pages feel patronizing, and it's a tell that the redesign is decorating rather than simplifying. If the IA folks land on different groupings, the labels and helper lines port across cleanly; the presets are the piece most coupled to the final toggle set, so re-check those last.

One question back: are any of these 12 toggles actually *account-level* vs *device-level*? If sync, theme, or "stay signed in" differ in scope, the copy needs a small scope hint ("on this device" / "on all your devices") that I've only partly threaded in — worth a pass once the IA agent's grouping lands.
