# Mosaic response

**Task:** Should we rewrite our 50k-line Python monolith in Rust? Team is 4 engineers, 2 are Rust-curious but haven't shipped Rust at scale. Performance is occasionally an issue but not a daily pain. We have ~12 months of runway.


## technical_feasibility_and_cost *(voice: codex)*

I couldn’t ground this in the actual monolith because `/private/tmp` does not contain a repo, so treat this as scenario-based technical sizing. My feasibility read: a full rewrite is not a 12-month project for this team unless the monolith is unusually simple and very well specified. A 50k-line Python system will likely become something like 60k-100k lines of Rust once explicit types, ownership boundaries, error enums, serializers, tests, adapters, and migration glue are added. For a 4-engineer team with no one having shipped Rust at scale, I’d model the first 2-3 months as ramp plus architecture, then maybe 500-1,200 production Rust LOC per engineer-month including tests, review, debugging, integration, and parity work. That yields roughly 18k-45k durable Rust LOC in 12 months, not a complete replacement.

The hard part is not syntax translation. Python idioms move poorly: duck typing becomes traits/generics/enums and forces domain modeling decisions; dynamic dispatch becomes `dyn Trait` or enum dispatch with explicit lifetime and ownership consequences; GIL-bound concurrency does not automatically become fast Rust concurrency without redesigning shared state; runtime metaprogramming, decorators, monkeypatching, reflection, and dynamic imports often need architectural replacement. Every boundary becomes work: choosing `tokio` vs `async-std` or staying sync, redesigning exceptions into `Result`/`thiserror`/`anyhow`, adding `serde` schemas at every API/storage/message boundary, and using FFI/PyO3 or Rust equivalents for any C-extension or Python-only dependency. Those are product-risk items, not just implementation details.

The performance claim needs to be narrowed before any rewrite decision. If the pain is CPU-bound in 5% of the code, Rust extension modules via PyO3, Cython, mypyc, NumPy/vectorization, or targeted algorithm changes are likely much cheaper. If it is I/O-bound, Rust may not help much compared with async architecture, DB/index/query work, connection pooling, caching, batching, or queue design. If it is memory-bound, profiling allocations/object churn and data layout may justify selective Rust, but still not necessarily a rewrite. I’d insist on profiling first: wall-clock traces, CPU profiles, allocation profiles, DB timings, p95/p99 endpoint/job latency, and a ranked list of hotspots. “Python is slow” is not specific enough to justify spending the company’s runway.

| Assumption | Effective Rust LOC shipped in 12 months | Approx monolith coverage | End state if runway ends |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Slow/ramp-heavy: 500 LOC/eng-month, 36 effective eng-months | 18k | 20-30% | Two systems, duplicated models, incomplete parity, likely higher ops burden |
| Moderate: 800 LOC/eng-month, 40 effective eng-months | 32k | 35-50% | Partial Rust core plus Python shell, migration risk still active |
| Optimistic: 1,200 LOC/eng-month, 42 effective eng-months | 50k | 55-80% | Maybe substantial subsystem replacement, but still integration/test debt |
| Very optimistic: 1,800+ LOC/eng-month | 75k+ | 80-100% | Requires strong Rust experience, stable specs, low product churn; does not match stated team |

My recommendation for the doer to synthesize: frame the rewrite as technically possible but economically mis-sized. The technically credible path is profile-first, then replace the narrow performance-critical slice: PyPy if compatibility is easy, mypyc/Cython for typed hotspots, PyO3 Rust modules for CPU/memory-heavy kernels, and architecture/database fixes for I/O latency. A full Rust rewrite should require evidence that the dominant bottleneck is systemic CPU/memory cost across a large fraction of the codebase, plus a migration plan that keeps the Python system shippable throughout.

## team_and_organizational_risk *(voice: claude)*

## Team & Organizational Risk

The math here is brutal and worth stating plainly: you have 4 engineers and ~12 months of runway. A Python→Rust rewrite of 50k lines, with half the team learning Rust on the job, is not a 12-month project — it's an 18–24 month project that you'll start telling yourselves is 9 months. The two Rust-curious engineers will be productive at maybe 40% of their Python output for the first 4–6 months; the two non-Rust engineers face a choice between (a) learning Rust under deadline pressure, which is the worst possible way to learn it, or (b) becoming the "maintain the dying Python system" team, which is a well-documented path to resignation within 6–9 months. Either way, you are looking at a real probability — I'd put it above 30% — that at least one of your four engineers leaves before the rewrite ships. Replacing them is the hiring question, and the hiring market for Rust engineers at what I'd guess is your comp band (you have 12 months of runway, so you are not paying Discord/Cloudflare rates) is genuinely thin. You will wait 3–6 months and likely compromise on seniority. Meanwhile the Python codebase still needs someone who remembers why `_legacy_handler.py` exists.

The second-system literature is unkind to this plan and you should sit with the specific case studies rather than waving at them. Netscape didn't just lose time to the rewrite — they lost the *market* during the rewrite, because Microsoft shipped while they refactored. Python 2→3 took the *community* a decade and that was with full-time core devs and no runway clock. The pattern that recurs is not "the rewrite failed technically" — rewrites usually succeed technically, eventually. The pattern is "the rewrite succeeded six months after the company that funded it ran out of money or relevance." And the stakeholder story is the part founders consistently underestimate: "we spent a year of runway rewriting working software in a trendier language because performance was *occasionally* an issue" is not a story that survives a board meeting, a down round, or a key hire asking what you shipped last year. If performance were a daily, customer-visible, churn-driving pain, you'd have a story. "Occasionally an issue" is not that story.

My recommendation for the doer to weigh: there is a version of honoring the team's Rust curiosity that doesn't bet the company. Identify the 1–3 genuinely hot paths in the monolith and rewrite *those* as PyO3 extensions — this is a 2–6 week project per path, gives the Rust-curious engineers real shipped Rust experience, delivers measurable wins you can point at, and leaves the monolith intact. Pair it with a "next new service ships in Rust" policy so greenfield work builds Rust muscle without legacy translation overhead. Carve out explicit learning time (a half-day a week is more honest than "20% time" which always becomes 0%). Revisit the full-rewrite question in 9 months when (a) you have more runway clarity, (b) the team has actually shipped Rust and knows whether they like it in production, not in tutorials, and (c) you have data on whether the PyO3 hot paths solved the performance complaints anyway — which, frankly, they probably will.

One question worth surfacing to the developer: *who is asking for this rewrite?* If it's the two Rust-curious engineers, that's a retention signal to take seriously but not by rewriting the monolith — by giving them Rust work. If it's the founder/CTO, dig into whether the real problem is performance or whether it's "the Python codebase feels gross and I want to start over," because those have very different solutions and only one of them justifies spending the runway.
