Yes — passing unit tests means *your* contract still holds, but date parsing tends to hide a separate contract per caller. Worth a quick caller-by-caller check before you ship:

**Billing.** Anything that touches money deserves the most paranoia. Verify timezone handling hasn't shifted (a date parsed as UTC vs. local can move a charge into a different billing period), and confirm behavior on edge inputs hasn't changed: nulls, empty strings, DST boundaries, leap days. If you have any "what got billed last month" replay or snapshot, run the old and new parser on the same inputs and diff.

**User-facing display.** Failure mode matters more than correctness here. If a malformed date used to render as "—" and now throws, that's a regression even if every test passes. Check what happens with garbage input, not just well-formed input.

**CSV export.** This is the one I'd worry about most quietly. Downstream consumers (spreadsheets, partner systems, customers' own pipelines) depend on the *exact string format* of what you emit. A refactor that changes `2026-05-15` to `2026-5-15`, or shifts to ISO 8601 with a `T`, or drops trailing zeros, is invisible to your tests but breaks someone else's import. Diff a sample export before/after.

A practical move: take a representative batch of real production date strings, run them through both old and new parser, and diff the outputs. That catches caller-contract drift your unit tests can't, because your tests encode what you *thought* the function should do, not what each consumer actually relies on.

Also worth checking: ambiguous formats (`03/04/2026` — March or April?), two-digit years, and locale assumptions, if any input source could produce those.

If all three diffs are clean, ship with reasonable confidence. If not, you've found exactly the thing the unit tests missed.
